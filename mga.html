Why MGA matters

Traditional bargaining — I ask for €200, you offer €120, we land at €160 — leaves value on the table. It assumes a fixed pie and turns every negotiation into a tug-of-war.

MGA, developed by the Harvard Negotiation Project, shows that when parties understand each other's underlying interests, they almost always discover options that both prefer over the halfway point. The method is widely used in labour relations, international diplomacy, environmental disputes, and everyday business negotiations.

MGA ≠ splitting the difference. It is a structured method for finding solutions better than any compromise — by expanding what's possible before deciding how to divide it.

The Framework

Four principles.
One method.

These four principles work together. Skip one, and you risk a deal that satisfies no one — or no deal at all.

01

Separate People from the Problem

Address the relationship and the substance as two distinct problems — never let personality become the battleground.

02

Focus on Interests, Not Positions

Ask why, not just what. The position is the stated demand; the interest is the need behind it.

03

Before evaluating, invent. Brainstorm broadly and resist the urge to close the deal prematurely.

04

Insist on Objective Criteria

Ground decisions in independent standards — market rates, expert opinion, legal precedent — not willpower.

Principle 01

Separate People from the Problem

Every negotiation involves two levels at once: the substantive issue (what you're trying to agree on) and the relationship between the parties. Mixing them is the most common reason talks break down.

When someone feels personally attacked or disrespected, they stop solving problems and start defending themselves. Separating the two means you can be firm on the issue while staying respectful of the person.

Positional bargaining trap
"You always overpromise."
"Your team is impossible to work with."
"I need that by Friday — no excuses."
vs
MGA framing
"The last two deadlines were missed — how can we prevent that?"
"I'd like to understand where the friction comes from."
"Friday is critical for us. What would help you meet it?"
In practice: hotel supplier dispute

The purchasing manager is frustrated that a linen supplier delivered late for the third time. Instead of accusing the supplier's representative of incompetence, she separates: "The late deliveries are a serious operational problem for us — can we look at your fulfilment process together to find the bottleneck?" The relationship stays intact; the problem gets solved.

  • →

    Acknowledge emotions before tackling the issue

    People need to feel heard before they can think clearly about solutions.

  • →

    Use "I" statements about impact, not "you" accusations

    "When X happens, it creates Y problem" rather than "You did X."

  • →

    Signal respect for the person, firmness on the substance

    Hard on the problem, soft on the people — not the other way around.

Principle 02

Focus on Interests, Not Positions

A position is what someone says they want. An interest is why they want it — the underlying need, fear, hope, or constraint driving that demand. Positions often seem incompatible; interests usually are not.

Visible ↑ Hidden ↓
Position (the "what")
"I want a 20% discount" "I need Friday delivery" "I won't accept less than €45/hour"
Interests (the "why")
stay within budget avoid stockouts before the weekend cover childcare costs feel valued / respected reduce risk maintain client trust

The magic question: "Why is that important to you?" — asked sincerely, not tactically — almost always surfaces an interest that opens new options.

Classic example: the library window dispute

Two readers argue about a window. One wants it open; the other wants it closed. Position deadlock. But their interests? One needs fresh air; the other dislikes the draught. Solution: open a window in the adjacent room. The position was fixed; the interest was not.

Principle 03

Generate Options for Mutual Gain

Most negotiators jump from "here's the problem" to "here's my solution." MGA inserts a critical step: deliberate invention of options before evaluation. This separates the creative act from the judging act — because premature judgment kills creativity.

  • 1

    Separate inventing from deciding

    Run a brainstorm where no option is judged. Write everything down. "Impossible" ideas often contain the seed of a workable one.

  • 2

    Look for trades across different valued items

    Party A values timing highly; Party B values price. A deal where B gets a better price and A gets faster delivery costs almost nothing extra — it just requires that you surface both interests first.

  • 3

    Expand before you divide

    Ask: what could we add to the agreement that makes this better for both of us? A bigger pie is always easier to split than a small one.

  • 4

    Invent options for the other side too

    What would make it easier for them to say yes? Understanding their constraints helps you design solutions they can accept.

Hotel contract renewal

A hotel wants to renew its contract with a catering supplier but the proposed price is 15% higher. Instead of just haggling on price, they brainstorm: extended contract duration (security for the supplier), guaranteed volume minimums, early payment terms, co-marketing on the hotel's website. The final deal costs the hotel less cash, costs the supplier less margin — and locks in a relationship both value.

Principle 04

Insist on Objective Criteria

When the facts are ambiguous and the interests legitimate, who decides? MGA says: neither party's willpower — but an independent standard both can accept as fair. Agreeing on the criteria before discussing the outcome removes ego from the equation.

Sources of objective criteria

Market rates Industry benchmarks Expert appraisal Legal precedent Scientific evidence Replacement cost Depreciation tables Collective agreement What a court would decide
Without objective criteria
"That's just not acceptable."
"Everyone in this industry charges more."
"We've always done it this way."
vs
With objective criteria
"The Dutch Hospitality Association's 2024 rate card shows the market average at €38–42/hr."
"Shall we use the ABN AMRO Hotel Index as our benchmark?"
"What would an independent mediator recommend here?"

Never yield to pressure — only to principle. If the other party pushes harder, ask: "What's your reasoning?" If they can't offer a principle, you don't need to move.

Negotiating Power

BATNA:
Your fallback,
your leverage.

Your Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement defines how strong your position really is — regardless of what you say at the table.

What is BATNA?

BATNA is what you will do if this negotiation fails entirely. It is not your bottom line (the minimum you'll accept) — it is your best realistic alternative course of action outside this negotiation.

Knowing your BATNA answers the most important question in any negotiation: when should I walk away?

Strong vs. weak BATNA

Strong BATNA
"I have options."

You can walk away without serious harm. This gives you confidence, reduces concessions, and signals credibility. The other party senses it.

Weak BATNA
"I have to make this work."

Desperation shows. You concede more than you should and may accept a deal worse than your alternatives — which defeats the purpose of negotiating.

Decision zone: when to accept a deal
Worse than BATNA — reject
Consider carefully
Negotiate — potential value here
Better than BATNA — accept

Never accept a deal worse than your BATNA. Never reject a deal substantially better than it.

Three things to do with BATNA

  • 1

    Know your own BATNA before you sit down

    If you haven't identified your alternatives, you can't judge whether any deal is worth accepting. Write it down. Be realistic — wishful alternatives aren't BATNAs.

  • 2

    Improve your BATNA before and during negotiations

    Contact other suppliers. Develop in-house capacity. Build options. The stronger your alternative, the more leverage you have — even if you never mention it.

  • 3

    Understand (and sometimes strengthen) their BATNA

    What will they do if this fails? If their alternative is bad, they need this deal more than they show. If it's strong, you need to make your offer more attractive.

Case: salary negotiation

Jana is negotiating her salary for a new hotel management role. Her BATNA: a confirmed offer from a competitor at €3,400/month. She doesn't need to reveal this — but knowing it means she won't accept anything below €3,400, and she negotiates confidently from there. When the hotel offers €3,200, she declines without panic. They counter at €3,500. She accepts. Her BATNA did the work.

ZOPA: The Zone of Possible Agreement sits between your BATNA and theirs. If no such zone exists — if both parties are better off walking away — no deal should happen, and that's the right outcome.
